Study guide

This developing draft contains 5 chapters in 2 sections. No programming experience is required. Read in order and attempt each application before opening its feedback. Later economic, historical and governance sections remain in development; the full-book final memo is not yet part of the draft.

Choose a path

  • New to models: start with the two foundations chapters, then follow the data, adaptation and evaluation section.
  • Already familiar: use the foundations’ applications to check that you can distinguish context from training and assess a release’s materials and permissions. Then begin with data; use prerequisite links when needed.
  • Review a decision: revisit your record inventory, controlled experiment and evaluation note together. Check whether they describe the same task and conditions.

Reading-completion marks and quiz scores stay in your browser. A correct quiz does not establish mastery of a chapter’s written task. Optional disclosures add depth; core tasks need no coding, external documents or JavaScript.

0. How Language Models Work

  • Explain how training changes a model and how that model generates an answer.
  • Trace how prompts, retrieved documents and tools contribute to an application.
  • Identify the components and release materials needed to run, adapt or investigate a model.

Make: Trace one answer through tokenisation and generation, then propose changes for new Sunday hours, brief answers and live catalogue availability. Explain where information goes, whether parameters change, required materials and a test. Add one material needed to investigate original training.

Review: Explain the generation loop and fixed weights during ordinary inference. Distinguish request context, retrieval, training and tool execution; connect each intervention to necessary materials and an appropriate test. Explain a researcher’s additional need.

Application and feedback

1. What ‘Open’ Means

  • Distinguish a model release from a hosted application or model service.
  • Assess release materials and terms, including open weights and open-source AI.
  • Recommend a release and deployment route for a purpose, with unresolved checks.

Make: Assess Cedar and Rowan against the library’s adaptation and sharing requirements. Recommend a deployment route, cite the supplied terms, identify an unresolved practical question and a revision condition.

Review: Trace who operates the model; cite permissions and restrictions; distinguish absent from uninspected information; explain practical fit and a feasible next check.

2. Where Model Data Comes From

  • Distinguish data used in context, training and evaluation.
  • Explain how selection and provenance affect a dataset’s use.
  • Design a data split and identify missing coverage.

Make: Create a record-by-record data-use plan, a split rule and a coverage check.

Review: Distinguish context/training/test roles, provenance, permission and related families; do not infer performance from an inventory.

3. How Models Are Adapted

  • Choose an intervention at the context, application or parameter level.
  • Trace demonstrations, preferences and teacher–student signals.
  • Design a comparison that addresses alternative explanations.

Make: Annotate a teacher–student pipeline and design a controlled comparison.

Review: Locate weight changes, keep non-training changes visible and address confounders and regressions.

Application and feedback

4. How to Judge a Model

  • Specify evaluation conditions and score outputs under a rule.
  • Calculate and interpret a task-weighted comparison.
  • Recommend a next step with counterarguments and uncertainty.

Make: Write a 250–400 word decision note using the fictional task comparison.

Review: Reproduce both means, justify priorities, explain limited evidence and represent an alternative next step.

Application and feedback

Review the section as a whole

Can another reader trace your recommendation back through the scoring rule, comparison conditions and source records? Correct the earliest weak link, then revise the conclusion. An explicit reason to defer can be as sound as a bounded proposal to proceed. The new evaluation chapter includes two defensible responses and a review rubric.

What comes later

Each section will contain at most three chapters, and the order may change as its prerequisites become clearer. These are planned areas, not links to unwritten lessons.
