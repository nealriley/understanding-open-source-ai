A structured course on open language models: how they work, why anyone builds them, where they came from, how far behind the frontier they really are, and why Washington and Beijing both care. Ten chapters, an interactive timeline, a glossary, and an annotated bibliography of the sources.

How to use this book

Use the study guide to choose a reading path, make a short piece of work for each chapter, and build towards a decision memo. It includes self-check criteria and a fictional case you can analyse without chasing changing model rankings.

Read it front to back if you are new to the field: the two Primer chapters give you the vocabulary, and everything after that builds on them. If you already know how transformers and RLHF work, start at Chapter 2. Each chapter ends with a short quiz and a completion toggle; progress is saved in your browser only. Hover any dotted term for a definition.

The spine of the book is Nathan Lambert's open-source AI reading list (Interconnects, updated 13 September 2026). The essays supply arguments to examine, alongside primary research and release documents. The bibliography distinguishes inspected support from further reading; unresolved claims stay in the authoring record.

Who this is for

  • Engineers and product people deciding whether to build on Qwen, Kimi, GLM, Llama or a closed API, who want the strategic picture rather than a benchmark table.
  • Policy staff and journalists who need the technical grounding to evaluate claims about distillation, cyber risk and "banning" open models.
  • Students and researchers who want a narrative map of the field before diving into the papers.
A note on voiceMuch of the source material is opinionated essay writing, especially Lambert's. This book tries to separate three things clearly: what happened (dated events), what the evidence shows (papers and measurements), and what particular authors argue. Callouts marked Debate present positions the field has not settled.
