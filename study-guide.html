Study guide: from reading to a defensible decision

Use the book to explain a model release, evaluate a claim, and make a recommendation that someone else can check. Keep a short notebook of your answers; a completed chapter or quiz score is a reading record, not a qualification.

Choose a starting point

No coding is required for this reading path. You should be comfortable reading a table, following a citation, and explaining your reasoning in a paragraph. Begin with Chapters 0 and 1 if weights, inference, and release permissions are unfamiliar.

  • Full course: work through Chapters 0–9 and the tasks below. The chapter order builds vocabulary before comparison and judgment.
  • Engineering or product focus: start with 0 → 1 → 2 → 6 → 7; add 3 and 8 before completing the decision memo.
  • Policy or journalism focus: start with 0 → 1 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 8; use 6 to interrogate quantitative claims, then write the memo.

These shorter routes are starting points. Use each unit's prerequisites to fill gaps. Reading-time estimates exclude investigation and written work; choose a manageable session and stop at a task boundary.

A repeatable study cycle

  1. Try explaining the central idea before reading; note what you cannot yet explain.
  2. Read one section and record a claim, its source, and its limit.
  3. Attempt the unit task before opening its self-check.
  4. Correct your answer and record the reason for the change.
  5. In a later session, revisit one earlier distinction and apply it to a new case.

The chapters combine technical explanations, dated reporting, and arguments. Treat those as different kinds of material. For a current decision, inspect the original document and date; an attributed claim is not independent confirmation. Chapter 1 has been revised around this method; the tasks below help you apply it across the existing book.

0. How Language Models Work

Preparation: None; begin here. Read the chapter.

Your target: Explain what is learned during training and what happens during inference.

Make something: Draw a path from training data to weights to a generated response. Mark where a tokenizer, post-training, and inference fit.

Self-check and revision

Your explanation distinguishes data, learned parameters, and execution. Treat reinforcement learning as a possible post-training method, not a mandatory separate stage for every model.

Identify one sentence to improve. Return to its source or chapter section, revise it, and record what changed.

Final task: a one-page decision memo

Invented scenario: a small organisation needs an assistant to draft answers from internal manuals. Every answer will be reviewed by a staff member. Its sensitive documents must remain on its own servers; approved public documents may be sent to a hosted service. The organisation has limited operations capacity. It has not yet tested answer accuracy on its own questions.

Fictional options — exercise facts, not real products
OptionKnownUnknown
A: hosted serviceAPI access; provider runs the systemTask accuracy, contractual retention terms, total usage cost
B: downloadable modelWeights and inference code; terms expressly permit this internal use; no training data suppliedTask accuracy, required hardware, maintenance effort
C: research releaseWeights, training code, data documentation, and checkpoints; terms permit this internal useTask accuracy, deployment effort, whether additional transparency benefits this task

Recommend an option, a staged comparison, or a justified deferral. Separate sensitive and public-document uses. Do not invent missing prices, performance, or contract terms.

  1. State the intended task, constraints, and recommendation.
  2. Describe the release and permissions without relying on a label.
  3. Propose a task-specific evaluation and an acceptable-result criterion before comparing models.
  4. Compare operating effort, cost information needed, and risk controls.
  5. Give the strongest counterargument and a condition that would change your recommendation.
  6. Cite at least two relevant primary sources from the book; explain which principle each supports. Record the document date and any limits. Keep scenario facts separate from external evidence.
Review criteria and example reasoning

A sensible response could test B locally first for sensitive documents while checking whether available hardware and maintenance capacity suffice. C deserves comparison if research transparency serves a defined requirement; it is not automatically better. A may be considered for approved public documents, subject to evaluation and contract review. Deferring deployment can be reasonable if the task or operating constraints cannot be met.

None of these choices earns credit by itself. Review whether the memo respects the data constraint, describes the evidence accurately, defines a useful comparison, addresses an alternative, and makes its uncertainty actionable. If a criterion is missing, revise before considering the task complete.

Revisit: after another chapter or a later reading session, change one assumption (for example, available hardware or document sensitivity) and explain whether your recommendation changes.
