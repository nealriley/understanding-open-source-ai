Risk, Safety and the Data Commons

“Is it safe to release?” is incomplete until we specify a capability, an actor, a setting and an alternative. Open access can change both misuse opportunities and the ability to inspect or defend systems. The comparison is the substance of the argument.

Prerequisite check. From Chapters 0 and 1, distinguish a model’s capability from the access arrangement. Explain why publishing weights changes provider control without determining every downstream use.

By the end

  • Analyse a release against an explicit baseline and threat model.
  • Distinguish observed misuse, capability tests and marginal risk.
  • Evaluate data-access constraints without confusing availability with permission.

Real-world cases reviewed 16 September 2026. Publication dates identify historical evidence, not current rankings or rules. Practice cases are fictional.

Start with the alternative world

Kapoor and colleagues’ 2024 position paper proposes analysing the marginal risk of open foundation models: the additional risk relative to existing technologies. Its abstract reports insufficient evidence to characterise several misuse risks effectively. That is a reason to improve evidence, not a finding that the added risk is zero.

Suppose a fictional community is considering releasing a model that helps explain software defects. A relevant baseline might include existing search, prior downloadable models and hosted services. Compare the same actors and tasks in both worlds. If the new model makes a difficult task easier for novices, that matters even when experts could already perform it. If a service already supplies similar help, the incremental effect of downloadable weights may concern scale, cost or removal of monitoring rather than entirely new capability.

A threat model names the actor, goal, resources, access and target environment. “Someone could misuse it” is too broad to select a mitigation. Conversely, the existence of an alternative does not automatically eliminate added risk: lower friction or wider access can change the number of attempts and who can make them.

Toner: use the interval to prepare

In her April 2025 essay, Helen Toner argues against relying on indefinite nonproliferation of dangerous capabilities. She proposes using an adaptation buffer to improve resilience before capabilities become broadly accessible. She allows a role for restrictions within a wider strategy; her argument does not say that every temporary control is useless.

A counterargument asks whether some capabilities could cause severe harm before defences adapt. The policy choice depends on the likely benefit of delay, the feasibility of controlling access and the defensive work that delay enables. A buffer has no automatic value if nobody uses it to prepare. Equally, an assertion that adaptation will eventually happen does not establish readiness today.

Observed incidents are a selected record

Florian Brand’s incident survey argues against a simple association between open weights and observed misuse, drawing attention to reported abuse of hosted systems. Read it as a challenge to one-sided reasoning. The reviewed survey does not provide a population-normalised estimate proving that one access model is universally safer.

Reports differ in visibility. A hosted provider may detect and disclose activity that an independent deployment never reports. More incidents could reflect more use, stronger detection, more capable systems or genuinely higher risk. Fewer observed incidents could reflect less exposure or poorer visibility. To estimate a rate, you need a denominator and a detection model; to estimate added risk, you need a counterfactual.

This limitation cuts both ways. It prevents declaring broad safety from a quiet incident record and prevents declaring broad danger from a selected list of failures. Evidence of an incident can establish that a failure mode is possible without telling you its frequency across all deployments.

Thinking Machines: combine model and ecosystem readiness

Thinking Machines Lab’s July 2026 proposal combines model-level safety work with ecosystem readiness and staged access. Its discussion treats removing hazardous knowledge as an open research question rather than a demonstrated general solution. The proposal offers criteria to investigate, not certification that every model released under such a process is safe.

For a fictional release, stages might allow external evaluation before broader availability. Specify what evidence would permit advancing, what would pause release, and who reviews the judgement. Test the relevant modifications and deployment settings where feasible. A staged process is useful only if the evidence can change the decision; a sequence of dates with automatic progression supplies little assurance.

Once weights are widely copied, a provider cannot rely on the same revocation mechanism as a hosted account. Hosted access, however, does not guarantee perfect monitoring or prevention. Compare actual controls and their failure modes instead of treating either distribution method as a complete safety policy.

Data is also a governance constraint

Longpre and colleagues’ July 2024 “Consent in Crisis” study examines changing web-domain restrictions in 2023–24 and inconsistencies between expressed preferences in site terms and robots.txt. The reviewed abstract supports a bounded historical finding about restrictions in studied corpora. It does not decide the legality of every training use or show that the whole web has become unavailable.

Separate four questions: can the material be accessed, what permissions or terms apply, what personal or sensitive information it contains, and whether it is suitable for the task. A publicly reachable page is not automatically an unrestricted training resource. A permissive component licence also does not resolve every issue in a mixed dataset.

For the fictional community release, document provenance and exclusions, provide the information needed to assess reuse, and identify unresolved questions before treating the dataset as usable. More transparency can help scrutiny while raw redistribution may create its own problems. Chapter 1’s artefact-and-permission assessment remains essential.

Optional depth: why an incident count is not a causal estimate

An observed count depends on exposure, harmful attempts, success probability and reporting. Changing any of these can change the count. A causal design might compare matched actors performing controlled, nonharmful proxy tasks with and without the model, then examine how well the proxy represents real conditions. Such a study still needs explicit limits; it is not a direct count of prevented harms.

Put the argument to work

For the fictional defect-explanation model, write a release assessment naming an actor, a harmful goal at a high level, existing alternatives, possible added capability or scale, a mitigation, and the evidence still needed. Include one potential defensive benefit and one data-permission uncertainty. Do not provide instructions for misuse.

Compare your reasoning with the feedback

A good answer might compare novice access before and after release, including hosted alternatives, and ask whether independent deployment meaningfully changes skill requirements or monitoring. A staged evaluation can test an uncertain capability; defensive access may improve auditing. Neither benefit cancels the risk automatically. Data provenance and terms require their own review. Grade the explicit baseline, mechanism, evidence and uncertainty, not a predetermined release-or-withhold conclusion.
