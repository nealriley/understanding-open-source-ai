How to Judge a Model

The library has prepared its notices and designed a comparison. Someone returns with a chart: Alder scored 60%; Birch scored 70%. “Choose Birch,” they say. But what was counted, what did the systems receive, and which mistakes matter to the library? A result becomes useful when we can answer those questions.

This chapter brings the section together. We will score answers, calculate a small comparison and write a conditional next step. All library outputs, system names and numbers below are fictional. Alder and Birch name complete evaluated systems, not measured commercial models. The real research case explains an evaluation approach, not a current ranking.

What you will be able to do

  • Specify an evaluation’s task, inputs, conditions and scoring rule.
  • Calculate and interpret a weighted comparison without hiding per-task results.
  • Explain uncertainty and recommend a next step that the available evidence can support.

Before you start: does an improvement after retraining show that the new training data caused it? Only if the comparison addresses other relevant changes. Revisit baselines and controls. Also recall why a reserved test set should not guide repeated development choices.

1. Write down what the result is meant to establish

An evaluation is a procedure for examining performance against specified criteria. A metric turns some aspect of that performance into a measurement. A benchmark supplies a defined collection of tasks and an evaluation procedure for comparison. These are tools for answering questions; they do not decide which question matters.

The library’s question is whether a public-notice assistant can give supported answers under a bounded pilot. A researcher might instead ask whether a training method improves a particular capability. A procurement team might compare complete services, including speed and operating work. The same score need not answer all three questions. Stanford’s CS336 evaluation lecture distinguishes the inputs, model interaction, scoring and interpretation, and asks whether the object is a model or a larger system.

Write a short evaluation contract before viewing the results. This is our name for the record of the question and conditions, not a legal agreement:

  • Task and coverage: which visitor questions, documents, languages and exceptions are included?
  • Object: which model/checkpoint, application version and retrieval collection are tested?
  • Conditions: which instructions, tools, output limits, attempts and generation settings are allowed?
  • Scoring: what counts as success, who judges it, and how are disagreement and failed requests handled?
  • Decision: what observation would justify another internal test, a bounded pilot or deferral?

State whether conditions are held equal to investigate a model difference or deliberately differ to compare practical system choices. Both can be useful. Trouble comes from performing one comparison and claiming the other. If Birch receives a calculator and Alder does not, a difference on arithmetic is partly a system-design question.

2. Score actual outputs before trusting an average

The library supplies N-17: Sunday opening is 12:00–16:00; returns are accepted at the desk during opening. It asks, “Can I return a book at three on Sunday?” The agreed rule requires an answer supported by the notice, a source identifier and no unsupported addition. Concision is recorded separately.

The last answer is not necessarily bad in every setting. It fails the stated rule. If the rule is too strict for visitors, change it deliberately and evaluate under the revised rule; do not quietly change it for the preferred system. Preserve both the outputs and the rule version so another reviewer can understand the result.

For questions the notices do not answer, a well-founded “the supplied notices do not say” may be the desired result. An assistant that always declines could nevertheless fail every answerable question. Track both kinds of case. Similarly, a timeout is part of the complete service’s performance. Excluding failed requests from the denominator without saying so can inflate a success rate.

Human reviewers can disagree. Ask them to identify the specific criterion at issue, examine a shared sample, and record how differences were resolved. A model used as a judge is another measurement procedure to check against suitable human judgement. Fluent rationales from the judge do not remove the need to inspect its errors or sensitivity to answer order.

3. Keep the counts beside the score

Our fictional comparison has two categories: answers from manuals and arithmetic questions. Each system attempts the same ten cases in each category, once, with a fixed setup. The manual rule checks support and required citation; the arithmetic rule checks the correct final answer. All attempted cases remain in the denominator. These deliberately tiny counts teach calculation; they are not evidence of deployment readiness.

Alder passes eight manual cases and four arithmetic cases. Birch passes six manual cases and eight arithmetic cases. Divide successes by attempts: Alder’s category rates are 80% and 40%; Birch’s are 60% and 80%. With equal importance for the categories, Alder averages (80 + 40) ÷ 2 = 60%; Birch averages (60 + 80) ÷ 2 = 70%.

Because both categories contain ten cases, this equal mean also equals each system’s success rate over all twenty attempts. If the category sizes differed, an equal-category average and a pooled success rate would generally be different. Keep the denominator visible and say which average you report.

4. Explain why two estimates disagree

Suppose, for this exercise, the library assigns manual answers 80% of the weight and arithmetic 20%. Then Alder scores 0.8 × 80 + 0.2 × 40 = 72%. Birch scores 0.8 × 60 + 0.2 × 80 = 64%. Alder now leads. No underlying answer changed; the summary expresses different priorities.

A weighted mean multiplies each category’s score by its assigned share of the total and adds the results. The weights here sum to one. They could represent an estimated request mix, an explicitly chosen policy priority or a scenario to explore. Those interpretations are different. In this fictional example they are stipulated decision weights, not observed library traffic.

The systems tie when manual answers receive two-thirds of the weight. Above that point Alder has the larger weighted score; below it Birch does. This sensitivity matters if the library does not know its future task mix. Report the disagreement honestly: the equal mix favours Birch, while the manual-heavy mix favours Alder. Neither calculation by itself identifies the correct priorities.

A single average may also be the wrong decision rule. If an invented rule exception is unacceptable in the proposed pilot, the team might require a separate threshold for that failure type. High arithmetic performance should not silently compensate for unsupported policy answers. Decide the constraint before choosing the winner; this chapter supplies no universal acceptable error rate.

Optional: derive the crossover

Let w be the manual weight between 0 and 1. Alder’s score is 80w + 40(1 − w) = 40 + 40w. Birch’s is 60w + 80(1 − w) = 80 − 20w. Equating them gives 60w = 40, so w = 2/3. This is exact arithmetic on invented counts. It does not estimate uncertainty about either system’s real performance.

5. Ask how easily the conclusion could change

There are several distinct uncertainties. Sampling uncertainty concerns which cases entered the test. Run variation concerns different outputs from repeated execution. Measurement uncertainty concerns the scoring rule and reviewers. Coverage uncertainty concerns situations the test omitted. More repetitions of the same easy question do not resolve all four.

In a category with ten cases, changing one outcome moves its rate by ten percentage points. At an 80% category weight, that moves the weighted total by eight points. If one additional Alder manual answer failed, its weighted total would fall from 72% to 64%, tying Birch’s original result. That is a sensitivity calculation, not a confidence interval or an estimate that such a change is likely.

A confidence interval is a range produced by a statistical procedure under stated assumptions. It expresses a particular kind of uncertainty, not permission to ignore a badly chosen test set. Related cases, repeated prompt selection and changed conditions complicate interpretation. Here we do not attach an interval to invented observations or treat a small arithmetic lead as significant evidence.

The idea of generalisation connects this to training. We care about new relevant cases, while we observe a finite set. Dive into Deep Learning distinguishes training error from performance on an underlying distribution. In practice, describe the population of requests your test is intended to represent and where that description may fail.

For Alder, follow-up evidence could include additional checked notice families, varied question wording, repeated runs with recorded settings, and independent scoring of disputed answers. Keep a final evaluation separate from the cases used to choose improvements. For an external release whose original training data are unavailable, explicitly record that overlap with its pretraining cannot be fully inspected.

6. A benchmark can make its limits visible

Research case · reviewed 16 September 2026

Liang and colleagues’ Holistic Evaluation of Language Models (HELM) explains an approach based on scenarios, multiple metrics and standardised comparisons. Its introductory discussion explicitly identifies missing coverage and the limits of comparison, including unequal training resources and possible train–test overlap.

That is useful even when we do not reuse its particular tasks. A report can state what it measures, which conditions it controls and what it leaves out. “Holistic” is an aspiration made inspectable, not proof of completeness. We use this historical methodological case without reproducing its model rankings as present-day guidance.

When someone brings a benchmark chart to the library, ask for the model versions, evaluation date, prompts, tools, attempts, scoring procedure and cases relevant to the intended job. Missing information does not automatically make a result false. It narrows the conclusions you can defend and identifies a useful next investigation.

Write a section decision note

Use the fictional table above and these additional stipulations: the pilot concerns public notices; the library proposes an 80% manual-task weight; the inspected release terms permit its intended pilot; hardware feasibility has not been checked; and there are no separately reported results for missing-information cases. No model may access visitor accounts in this exercise.

Write 250–400 words recommending the next step. Include:

  1. Your calculation under equal weights and the proposed 80% manual weighting, explaining the disagreement.
  2. A data-coverage gap and a check on the provenance or independence of test cases.
  3. A baseline and one controlled intervention to investigate.
  4. A serious reason to choose differently, an uncertainty and an observation that would change your recommendation.
Two defensible responses, and how to review yours

Response A: investigate Alder in a bounded internal trial

“The proposed manual-heavy task mix favours Alder, 72% to 64%, although the equal mix favours Birch, 70% to 60%. I recommend checking whether Alder can run on the available hardware and then conducting an internal public-notice trial. The calculation supports a candidate for investigation, not public deployment. The counts are tiny, and missing-information behaviour is unmeasured.

“Use a corrected-notice retrieval baseline and compare it with the same setup plus a cited-answer prompt. Keep the model, cases, tools and output budget fixed. Reserve new notice families and independently review expected answers, including cases that should decline. Birch remains plausible if the task mix changes. I would revise my recommendation if Alder invents rule exceptions on the reserved cases or proves impractical to operate.”

Response B: defer the model choice and strengthen the evidence

“I would first establish the task mix, hardware feasibility and missing-information scoring. Alder’s advantage at 80% manual weight rests on a priority choice and a small test; one changed manual outcome could erase that weighted lead. Birch wins the equally weighted exercise, so the results do not support a universal winner.

“Compare both systems and a simple notice-search baseline on the same independently checked questions. Keep notice revisions and derivative families traceable, and avoid using the final cases to tune prompts. If those checks show a useful advantage for either system under the library’s actual constraints, proceed to a bounded internal pilot. The strongest objection to waiting is the opportunity to learn from a small trial now; I would accept that trial if it is explicitly exploratory and does not expose untested answers to visitors.”

Review rubric

  • Evidence: correct counts, denominators and calculations; fictional results remain labelled.
  • Reasoning: weights and baseline match the proposed task; causal claims do not exceed the controls.
  • Counterargument: another course of action is represented fairly, with a reason it might win.
  • Uncertainty: identify a concrete missing observation and a condition for changing course.
  • Scope: permission, operational feasibility and task quality are assessed separately.

A different recommendation can succeed under these criteria. Revise an answer that claims either system is universally better, treats a generated test answer as checked evidence or assumes that a licence proves readiness.

You have followed data into a proposed change and followed outputs into an evidence-based decision. That gives later discussions of cost, competition and governance something concrete to rest on.
