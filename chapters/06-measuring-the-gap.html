Measuring the Gap

“How far behind?” sounds like one question. It can mean a score difference, a delay to a threshold, or a gap in useful work at a given cost. A good comparison begins by choosing which question matters.

Prerequisite check. Using Chapters 0 and 1, distinguish a checkpoint from a hosted product and weights access from capability. Explain why changing a tool harness could change an evaluation result.

By the end

  • Calculate a score and a threshold delay from a small documented example.
  • Explain how evaluation and aggregation choices change a comparison.
  • Specify evidence needed before applying a benchmark result to a decision.

Real-world cases reviewed 16 September 2026. Publication dates identify historical evidence, not current rankings or rules. Practice cases are fictional.

Choose the quantity before choosing the chart

A benchmark is a task collection and scoring rule. A result also belongs to a model version, prompt format, sampling configuration and environment. Giving one model tools or more attempts changes the comparison. A careful record includes those choices, the evaluation date, missing results and uncertainty. The country or licence of a model cannot substitute for this information.

A difference of ten percentage points is not a delay of ten months. A delay needs a threshold and dated observations. A cost comparison needs units: per token, per attempt, per successful task or full operating expenditure. If different tasks have different error costs, an average can hide the failure you most care about.

SemiAnalysis: compare within an era

SemiAnalysis’s “Are Open Models Catching Up?” divides its analysis into early scaling, reasoning and agentic periods. Its method uses selected benchmarks and model versions; the authors normalise scores and combine them within an era. They also caution that benchmark results need not match their own product preferences. This is a methodological argument for suitable tests, not proof of a universal halving law.

Changing the benchmark set may make each period more meaningful while preventing a straightforward comparison of the numerical indices across periods. Ask whether a reported trend survives different model selections, weights and baseline dates. The teaching data below are entirely fictional and are not a reconstruction of that article.

A transparent fictional comparison

Alder and Birch are fictional systems tested on ten manual questions and ten arithmetic questions, with one attempt each. Alder answers 8 manual and 4 arithmetic questions correctly; Birch answers 6 and 8. On an equally weighted average, Alder scores 60% and Birch 70%. For a manual-heavy workflow, weighting manuals at 80% gives Alder 72% and Birch 64%. The ranking reverses because the objective changes, not because either measurement changed.

Ten questions per task are a small teaching sample, not a reliable estimate of deployment performance. These exact percentages describe the invented results only. A real study needs representative held-out questions, repeated runs where outputs vary, and an account of sampling uncertainty. A higher mean does not establish that a small difference is robust.

Ihle: match a threshold across release dates

Håvard Tveit Ihle’s 28 May 2026 analysis measures time between closed and open models crossing selected score thresholds. He distinguishes backward-looking gaps from predictions about when a present leader will be matched. His caveats include missing model tests and possible serving-provider degradation. His results and SemiAnalysis’s era composites need not answer the same question.

In the fictional record below, Harbor reaches a fixed threshold in month 0; Meadow first reaches it in month 4. That is a four-month observed delay. On a different task, the first crossings are month 1 and month 7: six months. Their equal average is five months, but a manuals-only user cares about the four-month result. “Not yet observed” would be missing or censored data, never a zero-month gap.

Interpretation is another layer

A claim of perpetual catch-up can refer to a moving frontier: each time one model reaches an old threshold, another release moves ahead. A fixed-threshold study can report shorter delays while users still prefer a newer product. Before declaring two authors inconsistent, write their quantities as sentences with units, dates and populations. Then identify what evidence would actually contradict each sentence.

Optional depth: normalisation, uncertainty and missing data

For task scores x and y, a weighted mean is w×x + (1−w)×y. The fictional slider varies w, not the underlying observations. Normalising each benchmark by its observed maximum creates an index whose denominator changes if a stronger model enters the comparison. Do not interpret such an index as percent of all possible intelligence. Confidence intervals require a stated sampling procedure; invented error bars would be worse than an explicit limitation.

Read a dashboard as a dated method

A live dashboard can help locate candidate models, but preserve a snapshot and read its method before using a result in a memo. Record the evaluation version, inclusion rules, update date and price assumptions. This book does not load live results. Its fictional exercises remain reproducible as the market changes.

Choose for the task

Suppose one option is cheaper but makes more consequential errors. Neither is automatically best. A Pareto comparison identifies options that another option does not beat on every chosen objective; the decision still requires priorities. Evaluate cost per acceptable completed task and the burden of review. A model that leads a broad index might be unsuitable for a local, sensitive-document workflow.

Put the argument to work

Recalculate the equal and 80%-manual averages for Alder and Birch. Calculate both threshold delays. Explain why “Birch wins” and “Alder fits our manual-heavy workload better” can both follow from these data. Propose one further evaluation and one limitation that would enter your memo.

Compare your reasoning with the feedback

Equal means are Alder 60 and Birch 70. Manual-heavy means are 0.8×80+0.2×40=72 and 0.8×60+0.2×80=64. Delays are 4−0=4 and 7−1=6 months. The ranking depends on the task weights; the delays answer another question entirely. Test held-out manuals representative of your organisation, inspect consequential errors and repeat unstable runs. The invented sample cannot justify a claim about real models or market trends.
