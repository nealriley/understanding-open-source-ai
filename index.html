Open Models: A Field Guide to Open-Source AI

A structured course on open language models: how they work, why anyone builds them, where they came from, how far behind the frontier they really are, and why Washington and Beijing both care. Ten chapters, an interactive timeline, a glossary, and an annotated bibliography of the sources.

How to use this book

Use the study guide to choose a reading path, make a short piece of work for each chapter, and build towards a decision memo. It includes self-check criteria and a fictional case you can analyse without chasing changing model rankings.

Read it front to back if you are new to the field: the two Primer chapters give you the vocabulary, and everything after that builds on them. If you can already explain training versus inference and assess a release’s materials and permissions, start at Chapter 2. Each chapter ends with a short quiz and a completion toggle; progress is saved in your browser only. Hover any dotted term for a definition.

The spine of the book is Nathan Lambert's open-source AI reading list (Interconnects, updated 13 September 2026). The essays supply arguments to examine, alongside primary research and release documents. The bibliography distinguishes inspected support from further reading; unresolved claims stay in the authoring record.

0. How Language Models Work

Follow a library assistant from tokens and training to retrieval, tools and deployment. Worked examples explain which information and components change.

1. What 'Open' Means

Follow requests across deployment options, inspect a release package, and use its documents to assess access, permissions and practical fit.

2. Why Anyone Builds Open Models

A free download can be part of a profitable business, a research programme or a strategy against a rival. These explanations can coexist. The hard part is deciding what the evidence establishes about a particular release.

3. Risk, Safety and the Data Commons

“Is it safe to release?” is incomplete until we specify a capability, an actor, a setting and an alternative. Open access can change both misuse opportunities and the ability to inspect or defend systems. The comparison is the substance of the argument.

4. The Lineage of Open Models

The history of open models is a history of changing access to useful artefacts. A release can matter because it makes research possible, broadens deployment or demonstrates a training method—even when it does not top a benchmark.

5. US–China Competition

A country-level lead can describe downloads, deployment, benchmark scores or revenue. Those measures can move differently. To understand US–China competition, first identify the actors and the quantity being compared.

6. Measuring the Gap

“How far behind?” sounds like one question. It can mean a score difference, a delay to a threshold, or a gap in useful work at a given cost. A good comparison begins by choosing which question matters.

7. Distillation and Synthetic Data

Distillation describes an information flow. The same word can cover authorised model compression, synthetic training examples and allegations of abusive access. Keep the mechanism, the permission question and the measured benefit separate.

8. Security, Cyber and the Regulatory Fight

A policy can sound decisive while targeting the wrong mechanism. Compare proposals by the harm they aim to reduce, the actors they can reach and the evidence that would show whether they work.

9. Synthesis: Where This Goes

A defensible recommendation joins a task, a release, evidence and a revision condition. The book’s disagreements are useful because they expose different assumptions about value, control and risk. Your final memo should make those assumptions visible.

Who this is for

  • Engineers and product people deciding whether to build on Qwen, Kimi, GLM, Llama or a closed API, who want the strategic picture rather than a benchmark table.
  • Policy staff and journalists who need the technical grounding to evaluate claims about distillation, cyber risk and "banning" open models.
  • Students and researchers who want a narrative map of the field before diving into the papers.
A note on voiceMuch of the source material is opinionated essay writing, especially Lambert's. This book tries to separate three things clearly: what happened (dated events), what the evidence shows (papers and measurements), and what particular authors argue.
