The Lineage of Open Models

The history of open models is a history of changing access to useful artefacts. A release can matter because it makes research possible, broadens deployment or demonstrates a training method—even when it does not top a benchmark.

Prerequisite check. From Chapters 0 and 1, distinguish final weights, training code, data documentation and intermediate checkpoints. Give one research question that final weights alone cannot answer.

By the end

  • Compare two historical milestones using dated primary documents.
  • Explain what an artefact release enabled and what it left unknown.
  • Distinguish a publication date, a release date and later retrospective interpretation.

Real-world cases reviewed 16 September 2026. Publication dates identify historical evidence, not current rankings or rules. Practice cases are fictional.

First ask what kind of date you have

A paper submission, a product announcement and a downloadable checkpoint can appear on different days. Later revisions may document material absent from the first release. Record the event type before building a timeline. The milestones here are selected examples, not a complete lineage or a claim that later models simply descend from earlier ones.

The dates in this chapter attach to specific documents. A paper’s first submission provides a dated public record of what its authors reported; it does not automatically prove that every linked file was available at that moment. For a legal or reproducibility assessment, inspect the particular artefact version too.

OpenAI’s 5 November 2019 GPT-2 announcement identifies the final step in its staged release. This is a historical example of release as a process with changing access. It is not evidence that staged release always prevents misuse, nor that the same threshold would fit a later model.

Pythia: make development observable

The Pythia paper, first submitted 3 April 2023, describes a controlled suite with checkpoints across training and tools for reconstructing the data order. That changes the research question: investigators can compare development over time, rather than only inspect a final model’s outputs. The reviewed version is the 31 May 2023 revision.

Imagine studying when a fictional model begins repeating a particular pattern. Final weights let you test whether the pattern occurs now. Intermediate checkpoints let you investigate when it emerged. Controlling the data order helps interpret comparisons, but running the analysis still requires resources and a sound experiment. Access creates an opportunity to investigate; it does not supply a conclusion in advance.

OLMo: connect the weights to the training record

The OLMo paper, first submitted 1 February 2024, describes training data and training/evaluation code released alongside model weights. This broadens the material available for scrutiny. The inspected abstract is from the 7 June revision, so distinguish the initial milestone from the document version supporting the description.

Pythia and OLMo illustrate overlapping commitments to research access, with different project designs. Do not turn them into a contest over an undefined “most open” label. List what each document says is available, check the corresponding files and terms, then match them to a specific research task. A reader interested in deployment may prioritise compatible inference tools; a researcher studying data effects needs much more.

Llama 3.1: distribution and strategy meet

Meta’s 23 July 2024 announcement accompanied the Llama 3.1 family and a public argument for an open ecosystem. Read that strategy beside the Llama 3.1 Community License, not as a substitute for it. Chapter 1 explains why downloadable artefacts and release-specific permissions must remain separate.

A historically useful question is what a team could do after this release that it could not do under a hosted-only arrangement: operate a compatible checkpoint in its own environment, for example, subject to the actual terms and resources. That claim is more precise than saying the release “democratised AI,” which would also require evidence about who could afford and use it.

DeepSeek-R1: distinguish a method from a ranking

The January 2025 DeepSeek-R1 report describes RL-based development and smaller distilled models. Its place in this chapter is methodological: it makes a documented distinction between training a reasoning model and transferring behaviour to students. A claim of overall parity with another product would require matched evaluation conditions beyond the historical milestone.

A release can affect the questions researchers ask even if later models surpass its reported scores. Keep the durable contribution in the narrative and put changing performance comparisons in dated, method-specific cases. This avoids rewriting the entire history each time a new model appears.

From a list of names to an explanation

Across these examples, ask three recurring questions. Who gained a new kind of access? Which artefact made that access possible? What remained dependent on the releasing organisation or on substantial resources? These questions preserve the differences among staged publication, scientific transparency and commercial ecosystem building.

A chronology alone does not establish causation. If two releases occur close together, one might have influenced the other, both might draw on earlier work, or the timing might be unrelated. Establishing lineage requires explicit technical or documentary evidence, not a connecting arrow drawn because the dates are convenient.

Optional depth: archiving a release comparison

Record the document title, version, event date, retrieval/review date, precise passage and artefact link. If an announcement points to a mutable repository, prefer a release tag or commit for reproducibility. Keep later corrections in the record rather than silently replacing the original evidence.
