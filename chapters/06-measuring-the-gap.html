Measuring the Gap

"How far behind are open models?" sounds like a question with a number for an answer. In 2026 the honest answers range from three months to eighteen, and the spread is not sloppiness: it depends on which benchmarks you trust, whether you measure from release date or training date, and whether you think a benchmark measures anything a customer cares about. This chapter walks through the three serious measurement efforts and what each one is actually measuring.

What you will be able to do

  • Explain SemiAnalysis's three-era method and its headline result.
  • Explain Ihle's threshold method and why private benchmarks show a bigger gap.
  • Reconcile these with Lambert's "perpetual catch-up" and "different exponentials" positions.
  • Explain benchmark saturation, hill-climbing and the product-versus-benchmark distinction.

1. Why the number is contested

Three methodological choices drive most of the disagreement.

  1. Which benchmarks. Public benchmarks can be contaminated (the test data leaks into training) and hill-climbed (labs tune for them). Lambert's example: SWE-bench is "almost 3/4 Django", so a model can look like a great software engineer by being a great Django engineer. Private benchmarks avoid this but are fewer and harder to audit.
  2. Which dates. A closed model is typically trained months before release (GPT-4: 218 days). Measuring from release date flatters open models, whose Chinese builders ship within days of finishing training.
  3. Which direction. Do you ask "when did an open model first reach the level a closed model reached on date X?" or "on the day an open model was released, how old was the closed model it matched?" Ihle argues the second (backward-looking) is less biased.

2. SemiAnalysis: three eras, halving catch-up

SemiAnalysis's August 2026 analysis "Are Open Models Catching Up?" picks representative models and era-appropriate benchmarks, normalises for difficulty, and computes composite scores with open models run on vLLM and closed models pinned to API versions. Use the widget to explore each era.

The headline is that the time for open models to reach the closed frontier "halves with each new era": roughly 18 months in the scaling era (Llama-3.1-405B vs GPT-4o), 8.5 months in the reasoning era (R1-0528 vs the o1 line), and 4.8–6 months in the agentic era (Kimi K2.6 and GLM-5.2 vs Claude Opus 4.5). This happened even though frontier labs accelerated: the average interval between frontier releases fell to 51 days from 120–213. Two further claims matter for the rest of the book. First, the early open catch-ups (Llama 2, even R1) produced little economic value, whereas GLM-5.3 and Kimi K3 are "genuinely capable" at paid coding and agent work. Second, product beats benchmarks: Anthropic's ARR grew to "north of $65B" after Claude Code's May 2025 launch despite Kimi K3 matching or beating Claude on several benchmarks.

The authors' own caveats: benchmarks suffer memorisation and hill-climbing, are weak proxies for real work, and closed labs' safety-testing delays can inflate apparent catch-up speed.

3. Ihle: thresholds on 17 benchmarks

Håvard Tveit Ihle's May 2026 LessWrong post "How far behind are open models?" uses a different method. Across 17 benchmarks from the Epoch AI Benchmarking Hub (about 110 data points; 8 private, 9 public), he sets score thresholds at 5% intervals and measures the time between a closed model first crossing each threshold and an open model doing so, assigning each gap to the open model's release date.

The public/private split is the key finding: on evaluations labs cannot see, the gap is roughly double what the leaderboards show, and it has widened since the R1 moment. Ihle also notes that third-party inference providers sometimes serve open models in degraded configurations, and that on real-world tasks the gap is probably larger still, because open developers "may inadvertently optimize for benchmark-like tasks". He discloses that Claude Opus 4.7 assisted with code and research.

4. Lambert: perpetual catch-up, then compression

Lambert's position moved during 2026, which is itself informative.

  • February ("Open models in perpetual catch-up"): after GLM-5, the gap is a stable ~6 months and open models "are not meaningfully accelerating towards matching the best closed models in absolute performance". Public benchmarks compress complexity and the true gap is larger. Open models would need a 100×+ cost advantage to win the frontier outright.
  • March ("What comes next with open models"): "6–18 months behind", likely to widen, because modern AI is a system (tools, inference stack, interface) that closed labs vertically integrate. The opportunity is specialised small models, "10× faster, 100× cheaper", not frontier chasing.
  • June (GLM-5.2): 6–9 months, but GLM-5.2 is the first open model that "feels right" as a coding agent, which changes the economics.
  • July (Kimi K3): "compressed from 6–9 months to approximately 3–5 months".

The reconciliation is that the three sources measure different things. SemiAnalysis measures time-to-parity on era-defining public benchmarks, where Chinese labs' speed and benchmark focus show to best advantage. Ihle measures across a broad benchmark set including private ones, where hill-climbing is impossible. Lambert measures the felt experience of using the models in products, which lagged benchmarks until GLM-5.2 and then caught up fast. All three agree on the shape: a gap that shrank sharply at R1, widened during 2025, and compressed again in mid-2026 on the agentic axis specifically.

5. The live dashboards

Three public trackers let you check the current state rather than trusting an essay:

  • Artificial Analysis maintains an Intelligence Index and plots open-weights versus proprietary progress and US versus China over time. In its July 2026 evaluation, DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 scored 50, one point behind GPT-5.6 Luna, tied with Gemini 3.6 Flash, and seven behind Kimi K3 (57), at $0.14 per million input tokens.
  • Epoch AI's ECI benchmark can be filtered by accessibility (open vs closed) and by software-engineering tasks.
  • The Interconnects Adoption Dashboard tracks the ATOM Report's adoption series.

6. Frontier versus Pareto frontier

The reading list's own summary captures the resolution: open models "achieve the Pareto cost frontier, not the absolute performance frontier". At any capability level below the very top, the cheapest way to get it is an open model served competitively. This is a stable equilibrium as long as (a) closed labs keep a lead worth paying for on the hardest tasks, and (b) open labs keep releasing. Chapter 9 asks what happens to each condition.

Key ideaBenchmarks measure catch-up; revenue measures the frontier. Kimi K3 matched or beat Claude on several leaderboards in July 2026. Anthropic's revenue kept growing. The difference is the product, the harness, the reliability on long agentic tasks, and trust, none of which a static benchmark captures. Any gap estimate that ignores this will make open models look closer than customers feel they are.
Think about it: what would falsify "perpetual catch-up"?

Lambert's thesis predicts open models never lead in absolute terms. It would be falsified by an open model topping a broad, private, contamination-resistant evaluation for a sustained period, or by a closed lab exiting the frontier. SemiAnalysis's halving trend, extrapolated, reaches zero within two eras; whether the trend continues or floors at the safety-testing delay is the empirical question to watch.
