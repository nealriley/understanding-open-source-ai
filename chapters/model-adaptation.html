Chapter 3 · Data, Adaptation and Evaluation

How Models Are Adapted

Alder now receives the library’s current notices, but its answers are still troublesome. Sometimes it ignores the notice. Sometimes it answers correctly and then adds an invented detail. Sometimes it gives a page-long reply to a simple question. “Train it on our data” sounds like one remedy. These failures may need different changes.

In the previous chapter, we separated source documents, training examples and evaluation cases. Here we follow what those records actually do. Alder remains the fictional application, Cedar its underlying model. Our aim is to explain an intervention and the evidence needed to assess it—not assume that more training is always the next step.

What you will be able to do

  • Choose between a context change, an application change and a weight update for a stated problem.
  • Trace information through demonstrations, preferences, rewards and teacher–student training.
  • Propose a controlled comparison that distinguishes an intervention’s effect from other changes.

Before you start: a model writes a practice answer, a librarian checks it, and the team uses it in training. Which object supplies the update, and which data should remain outside that process? The checked example supplies the training signal; reserved evaluation cases should not become practice answers. Revisit data roles and data separation if needed.

1. Diagnose the failure before choosing a method

Adaptation broadly means changing how a model or system behaves for a purpose. Authors use the word at different levels. In this chapter, we specify whether an intervention changes the request, the surrounding application or trainable parameters. That is more informative than saying the system “learned.”

Imagine the application retrieves the obsolete Sunday notice. The first candidate fix is the retrieval collection or its selection logic. Retraining Cedar while still supplying the wrong notice would not directly repair that fault. If the notice is correct but Alder overlooks it, the team could first test clearer instructions and examples in the prompt. If the team wants a repeated response style across many tasks, training on demonstrations becomes another candidate. These are hypotheses to compare, not guaranteed cures.

Adding examples to a prompt is often called in-context learning. The examples influence the current computation; that phrase does not imply a permanent weight update. A conversation retained by an application may later supply examples for a separate training process, but storing a message and training from it are distinct events. Keep both routes visible when examining a product claim.

2. Show the behaviour: supervised fine-tuning

Supervised fine-tuning, or SFT, trains an existing model on examples of desired responses. The training system compares its predictions with target response tokens and uses the loss to adjust trainable parameters. This is the update process from Chapter 0, now applied to a selected task and format. A checkpoint after this process is a new version to identify and evaluate.

One fictional demonstration

Instruction
Answer from the supplied notice. Name the notice. If it is insufficient, say so.
Context
N-17: Sunday opening is 12:00–16:00; returns are accepted at the desk during opening.
Question
Can I return a book at 15:00 on Sunday?
Target response
Yes. N-17 says you can return it at the desk during Sunday opening, from 12:00 to 16:00.

A training collection needs more than many copies of this row. Include questions requiring an exception, questions where information is missing and varied wording. Otherwise the team risks teaching a narrow answer pattern while believing it has taught a general practice of checking evidence. A demonstration that always says “yes” supplies a very different signal from one that sometimes answers, qualifies or declines.

The format also matters. The runtime must distinguish instructions, visitor text and assistant responses in the way the model expects. Nathan Lambert’s instruction-tuning chapter makes the message format visible through chat templates. For our non-coding purpose, retain the distinction among roles; we do not need to write the template. A template alone does not guarantee that the model obeys the instruction.

A model can improve on the training examples without becoming reliable on new ones. A regression is a deterioration in a previously useful behaviour: shorter replies might omit a necessary exception, for example. Keep representative earlier tasks in development checks and in the final evaluation design. Success should name the behaviour improved and the behaviours the team also checked.

3. Compare responses: preferences and rewards

Sometimes a reviewer can choose between two responses more readily than write an ideal one. A preference pair records a prompt, two responses and which response is preferred under specified criteria. Those criteria matter. A preference is a judgement from a particular process, not a measurement of universal human agreement.

Same question, different judgement

Question: Can I return a book at three on Sunday?

A: Yes.

B: Yes. N-17 permits desk returns during Sunday opening, 12:00–16:00.

A reviewer instructed only to choose the shortest answer selects A. A reviewer applying the library’s evidence rule selects B. The training record needs the rule, not just a winner.

A reward is a numerical signal used by a training procedure to favour some behaviour. One route trains a separate reward model from human comparisons, then adjusts the language model to obtain higher predicted reward. This is one form of reinforcement learning from human feedback, or RLHF. The reward model approximates the collected judgements; its score is not proof that an answer is true.

Documented method · reviewed 16 September 2026

Ouyang and colleagues’ InstructGPT paper (2022), section 3.1, describes demonstrations for supervised training, comparisons for a reward model, and subsequent reinforcement learning. This is a concrete training recipe, not a compulsory sequence for every model. Its limitations section matters alongside its method: a trained assistant can still make mistakes.

Another route uses preferences without a separately trained reward model and the same reinforcement-learning loop. Rafailov and colleagues’ Direct Preference Optimization paper explains such an approach. DPO changes the model using preferred and dispreferred responses relative to a reference model. The key distinction here is the training signal and procedure; neither acronym guarantees quality on our library task.

Rewards can also come from a checker, such as whether an arithmetic answer matches a verified result. This differs from a human preference. In our fictional library, a checker that rewards every answer containing “N-17” could favour unsupported answers that merely cite the notice. The model would be optimising the specified signal; the signal would fail to express our full objective. Before trusting a score, inspect what earns it and what it ignores.

4. Learn from another model

Synthetic data is generated by a model or simulation rather than obtained directly as the event or observation it represents. In this project it could be a generated question, a draft answer or a proposed preference. Generation changes the origin of a record. It does not remove the need to check relevance, permission, errors and duplicates.

Distillation describes a teacher–student training relationship. A teacher model supplies information used to train a student. In a response-based arrangement, the teacher’s selected answers become targets. The student need not receive the teacher’s weights, and the teacher need not run when the resulting student later answers a user.

If the teacher invents the drop box, accepting the response gives the student a bad target. Asking the teacher to mark its own answer correct does not supply independent verification of that claim. A source check can reject it. The team must also investigate gaps: a teacher that only generates easy Sunday questions will not provide varied evidence by generating more of them.

Distillation and SFT are not mutually exclusive labels. A team can use SFT to train a student on teacher-written answers. One label identifies where the supervision comes from; the other identifies how the examples are used. Likewise, synthetic data can enter several training stages. To understand a release, trace the information rather than searching for one winning method name.

Optional: predictions contain more than one selected answer

Hinton, Vinyals and Dean’s distillation paper, section 2, describes transferring a distribution of class probabilities as soft targets. Unlike a single selected response, a distribution contains relative preferences among possible outputs. Whether a method is feasible depends on access to its required teacher signal, not simply access to a chat box.

Agarwal and colleagues’ on-policy distillation method uses student-generated sequences with teacher probabilities as guidance. This changes which sequences receive feedback. It is not the same as the teacher writing a fixed answer collection first. We use the paper to distinguish mechanisms, not claim a universal improvement.

5. Show what the change contributed

A controlled comparison holds relevant conditions steady while varying a specified intervention. In the library, “we trained the model and it improved” leaves many questions unanswered. Did the team also replace the notices, rewrite the prompt, change the answer budget or select easier questions? Any of those changes could contribute to the outcome.

First define a baseline: the arrangement against which the proposal is compared. It can be an existing model, the current application or a simple non-model solution. A notice-search page is a plausible library baseline if it serves the intended task. Comparing with an intentionally poor prompt would establish little about the best practical alternative.

For a training study, fix the starting student checkpoint, data eligibility rules, evaluation cases and runtime settings. Compare an unchanged student with a student trained on the proposed examples. To isolate the value of teacher-produced targets, add a comparison trained on equally eligible staff-written targets, matching the training budget as closely as the question requires. Record residual differences rather than claiming a perfect experiment.

A confounder is another difference that makes the cause of the result ambiguous. More training steps in the teacher-data condition could confound a claim about the data source. A causal claim says a change produced an effect; it needs a design that addresses such alternatives. A practical choice between complete systems has a different aim and may deliberately compare different recipes, provided it says so.

Design the library’s next experiment

The team proposes to generate answers with a teacher, train Cedar on them, replace an obsolete notice and double the maximum answer length. It will compare this with yesterday’s Alder and say that distillation caused any improvement. You have no measured results yet.

  1. Draw the path from source notice to teacher output to training record to student checkpoint. Mark where checking occurs and where weights change.
  2. Explain which proposed changes can improve the application without updating weights.
  3. Write four comparison conditions that can help separate the notice correction from training. State what stays fixed.
  4. Add one further comparison needed to investigate the value of teacher targets specifically. Explain one limitation and one regression to check.
Review the experiment before interpreting a result

The teacher generates text; the source check accepts, corrects or rejects it; training updates the student from selected records. Replacing the notice changes application context, and changing the output limit changes inference conditions. Neither alone is a weight update.

One useful four-condition design crosses old versus corrected notices with unchanged versus trained Cedar. Use the same test questions, scoring criteria and output budget for all four. This diagnoses the stale-notice contribution and whether training behaves differently under each context. An old-notice condition is an internal diagnostic, not a candidate for deployment with knowingly obsolete policy.

That design isolates the chosen training package more clearly, but does not show that teacher targets are better than other training data. Add a matched staff-target condition, keeping the starting checkpoint and training budget comparable. Review source quality, selection effort and duplicate families; teacher and staff datasets may still differ in ways that limit attribution. Repeated training runs would help examine variability.

Check that better citation or shorter style does not create more unsupported answers or omit exceptions. A defensible alternative postpones training and first tests the corrected retrieval baseline. No answer should invent an uplift from this proposal. A good plan specifies what would count as evidence and what would remain unresolved.
