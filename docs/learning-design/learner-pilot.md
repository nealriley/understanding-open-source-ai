# Learner-pilot protocol (prepared; not conducted)

## Purpose and participants

Test whether intended readers can distinguish training/inference, release access/permission, evidence/argument, and measurement/forecast, then use those distinctions in a conditional recommendation. Recruit separately from the mixed audience: engineers/product staff, policy/journalism readers and students/researchers. A small initial convenience pilot of 5–8 readers can reveal usability and explanation problems; it cannot estimate causal learning effects or represent every audience.

Participation is voluntary. Explain the purpose, allow breaks or withdrawal, and ask separately before recording. Use participant codes in notes, avoid collecting sensitive professional examples, and agree a retention period before sessions. Do not add analytics to the book.

## Session outline (proposed 60–75 minutes)

1. **Orientation (5 min):** record relevant prior knowledge and accessibility preferences. Explain that the book is being tested, not the participant.
2. **Before reading (5–10 min):** ask the reader to describe a fictional release and explain whether an improved answer means weights changed. Record reasoning without correcting it.
3. **Use the book (15–20 min):** assign the Chapter 1 release task or Chapter 6 calculation. Ask the reader to think aloud. Observe navigation, source inspection, table use, disclosures, keyboard access and confusion. Do not teach during the first attempt.
4. **Transfer (10 min):** present a different fictional release or weighting mix. Ask for an explanation and confidence. Separate answers obtained independently from answers after a hint.
5. **Memo planning (15 min):** use the study-guide case. Ask for a recommendation, strongest objection and revision trigger. Use the rubric to identify missing reasoning, not to enforce a preferred option.
6. **Debrief (10 min):** ask which passage helped, what remained unclear and where the reader mistook an argument or invented example for fact. Show relevant feedback after recording the initial attempt.

Offer a separate follow-up session for the complete written memo or delayed transfer. Do not rush a full ten-chapter course into this session.

## Observation and review

Use the companion sheet. Record task completion, explanation quality, source tracing, corrections, assistance and elapsed time; keep verbatim observations separate from interpretations. Note whether optional depth was needed to finish a required task. A reader needing it is a design problem to inspect.

Review each issue by severity: blocks task; induces a substantive misconception; slows navigation; or cosmetic. Prioritise repeated misconceptions and blocked tasks. Revise the relevant explanation, task or interface and recheck with a new case. Report participant count, backgrounds, assistance and limitations. Do not claim efficacy from satisfaction, completion or higher quiz scores alone.

## Proposed revision triggers

- A reader treats fictional chart values as reported data: strengthen labelling and context immediately.
- A reader cannot find source evidence or feedback: improve navigation and labels.
- Multiple readers repeat the same causal or legal misconception: revisit the explanation and feedback.
- Keyboard or narrow-screen use blocks a required task: fix before expanding recruitment.

These are pilot review rules, not validated thresholds for educational quality. Recruitment and actual sessions are separate work.
