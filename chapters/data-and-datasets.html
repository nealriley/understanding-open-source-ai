Chapter 2 · Data, Adaptation and Evaluation

Where Model Data Comes From

The library has inspected Cedar’s release and found a possible route to running its own assistant. Now someone asks, “Can we just give it our documents?” The librarian opens a folder containing a current notice, an old notice, a visitor’s message and an answer written by another model. All are text. They are not interchangeable.

We will continue with Alder, our fictional library application, using Cedar as its underlying model. The pilot’s job is to answer questions from public notices and identify the supporting notice. It should say when the supplied information is insufficient. It will not access visitor accounts or reserve books. Every library record and decision in this chapter is invented for teaching.

What you will be able to do

  • Distinguish documents supplied at answer time from examples used for training and evaluation.
  • Explain how selection, cleaning and provenance affect what a dataset can support.
  • Propose a defensible separation of development and test material, and identify missing coverage.

Before you start: does placing a notice in a request change the model’s weights? Does permission to download a model also settle permission to use a visitor’s message? If either distinction is unclear, revisit context and training and component permissions. In the ordinary inference route, weights stay fixed; the visitor message requires its own assessment.

1. Start with a record and a purpose

A dataset is a collection of records assembled for a purpose. A record is one item with fields that help us interpret it. It might be a whole document, a paragraph, a question paired with an answer, or two answers with a preference between them. A large collection of texts is also called a corpus. These names describe organisation, not quality.

The text tells us something about Sunday service. The other fields tell us which text we have and why the team thinks it belongs here. Those fields are metadata: information describing a record. Without the revision, an old and a current notice could look equally authoritative. Without the origin, a convincing generated sentence might be mistaken for a checked policy.

Now transform N-17 into a demonstration: question, “Can I return a book at three on Sunday?”; supplied notice, N-17; desired answer, “Yes. N-17 says returns are accepted at the desk during Sunday opening, from noon until four.” A reviewer can check each part. The transformation created a new record; it did not create a new independent source of the opening hours.

2. The same text can play different roles

In Chapter 0, pretraining used text to learn broad patterns. In the library project, a prepared answer might instead guide further training. Or a notice might enter only the application’s retrieved context. We must name the route because the practical consequences differ.

A retrieval collection is the set of material the application can look up. Replacing the Sunday notice there can change the next answer without retraining. A training collection influences the model through updates; it is not a directly editable file of facts inside the model. A test collection asks how the resulting model or application behaves. Merely labelling a folder “test” does not establish independence from training.

A label is an annotation associated with a record: a category, a preferred response or a reference answer, for example. It is not necessarily correct because a person supplied it. Two staff members could disagree over whether an answer is adequately supported. Recording the decision rule and the disagreement is more useful than treating the label as unquestionable truth.

3. A dataset is a series of choices

Curation means deciding what to collect, retain, transform and document. For the library, collection starts with the task: answering from public notices. A folder of thousands of visitor messages may be large but unsuitable for this pilot. A small set of checked notices may be more directly relevant. That does not prove it covers all the questions visitors will ask.

Consider four operations on the folder. Extraction takes useful text from its container, such as a web page. Filtering excludes records under a stated rule. Deduplication identifies repeated or closely related material. Mixing determines how much of each source or category reaches training. Tokenization, already introduced in Chapter 0, prepares the selected text for a particular model.

Each operation can change meaning. Removing repeated website menus could leave a cleaner notice. Removing every short sentence might also delete “Closed this Sunday.” Combining old and new notices without dates could create a contradictory collection. Keeping fifty near-identical Sunday questions might make that topic dominate practice while leaving renewals almost unrepresented. Record the rule and inspect what it keeps and removes.

Do not confuse a source’s share of stored files with its share of training exposure. A training process might revisit some records more often, or sample sources in different proportions. A file count, document count and token count answer different questions. Ask which quantity a dataset description reports and which quantity the training procedure actually uses.

Documented case · reviewed 16 September 2026

Soldaini and colleagues’ Dolma paper, sections 5.4–5.5, describes a web-data pipeline with URL and document deduplication, quality/content filtering and later paragraph deduplication. The authors explain why ordering matters: removing paragraphs too early can interfere with content analysis. This is a reported design, not a guarantee that every remaining item is suitable.

The dataset card separately lists versions and processing changes. “Dolma” alone therefore does not identify one immutable collection. Our lesson is to record the version and procedure, not infer a model’s quality from a corpus name. The card’s release prose and version table differ on the April 2024 v1.7 date; no exact release-day claim is needed here.

4. Trace the record, not just its filename

Provenance is the record of where material came from and how it changed. For a generated demonstration, keep the source notice, the generation instructions, the producing model/version if known, the filtering rule and the human check. For a copied notice, keep its origin, revision and extraction method. These details let a later reviewer investigate a mistake rather than guess.

Suppose the generated answer says books can go into an overnight drop box. N-17 never mentions one. A polished answer and a document reference are not enough; the cited document must support the claim. If the answer becomes a demonstration without checking, the team has turned an unsupported sentence into an instruction to imitate it.

Permission is a separate column in this record. The model’s licence covers specified model materials; it does not settle every question about the data a team later collects. For this exercise, the library has authorised its own public notices and has excluded private visitor messages. These stipulated facts let us practise selection without making a legal finding about a real corpus. An unknown permission should remain marked unknown until the relevant basis is checked.

This also explains an important distinction in openness. A team can publish useful information about sources, selection and transformations without giving every reader unrestricted access to every source item. Conversely, downloadable data with little documentation may be difficult to investigate. Return to the actual materials and permissions, as in Chapter 1, rather than awarding a single transparency score.

5. Protect the question you want the test to answer

Three data roles help organise an experiment. The training set supplies updates. A development set, also called a validation set, helps the team choose prompts, settings or model versions. A test set is reserved for evaluating the selected approach. Changing a design in response to test results makes those results part of development, even if no test record enters a training job. Google’s dataset lesson illustrates this repeated-use problem.

For Alder, group the original question and its generated paraphrases together before splitting. Otherwise almost the same demonstration might appear on both sides. This is a form of data leakage: information crosses a boundary intended to keep an evaluation meaningful. Randomly shuffling individual rows does not remove their shared origin.

The right boundary depends on the question. To ask whether Alder handles unfamiliar wording about known notices, test new questions while allowing the application to retrieve those notices. To ask whether it handles new policy documents, reserve entire notice families as well. Supplying a legitimate source document at answer time is part of a retrieval task; secretly supplying the reference answer is a different condition. State which question the experiment answers.

Neither a split nor a large total ensures coverage: inclusion of the situations that matter. The library needs ordinary requests, exceptions, missing information and different ways of asking. A later-date test can examine changed notices; a new-branch test can examine another library. These are proposed checks, not automatic promises of independence from a base model’s unknown pretraining history. Record what can and cannot be checked.

Optional: why there is no universal split percentage

A held-out set estimates behaviour on the cases it contains. Its useful size depends on variation, rare cases and the precision needed for the decision. If related records travel together, the number of independent groups can be much smaller than the number of rows. Do not treat a conventional percentage as evidence that a test is adequate. The later evaluation chapter shows how one changed outcome can move a small score.

Make a data-use plan

The library has six fictional records. Its current pilot permits public-notice answers only, with the permissions stated below. Decide use now, inspect first, or exclude from this pilot for each record. Then assign the usable material a role: retrieval source, possible training demonstration, development case or final test case. You may propose generating additional records; you cannot declare the present six sufficient.

Explain which records must be treated as related. Specify whether your proposed check concerns new wording or new notices. Name two missing kinds of case and one piece of metadata to preserve. Finally, write one claim about performance that this inventory cannot establish.

Compare your plan with the evidence

Use N-17 as the current retrieval source. Exclude N-03 from current answers; retaining it in a labelled historical archive is different from feeding it to the pilot as current policy. D-01 is a possible demonstration or development example. Do not use the same record to train and then claim an independent final success.

Inspect D-02 and remove or correct its unsupported claim before considering it as a demonstration. It remains part of D-01’s derivative family. Exclude V-01 from this pilot. Its availability does not override the stated scope and authorisation.

Q-01 can help test new wording or boundary reasoning about a known notice, if kept out of development. It cannot demonstrate performance on unseen notices. If that is the objective, collect different document families and reserve them. All N-17-based records need a family identifier even though Q-01 was written independently.

A cautious plan first tests retrieval on checked, public material. A later training plan collects demonstrations and reserves separate evaluation cases. Both should add renewal questions and missing-information cases, preserve revisions and origin, and avoid claiming that the inventory proves accuracy or complete coverage. Revise your plan if it relies only on a random row split or a larger count of Sunday paraphrases.
