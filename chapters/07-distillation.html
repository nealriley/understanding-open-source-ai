Distillation and Synthetic Data

Distillation describes an information flow. The same word can cover authorised model compression, synthetic training examples and allegations of abusive access. Keep the mechanism, the permission question and the measured benefit separate.

Prerequisite check. Sketch the training/inference distinction from Chapter 0. From Chapter 6, explain why two unequal training runs cannot isolate the effect of one data source.

By the end

  • Trace teacher–student information flow and locate the training stage.
  • Separate a company allegation from demonstrated causal uplift.
  • Design a controlled comparison of teacher-assisted training.

Real-world cases reviewed 16 September 2026. Publication dates identify historical evidence, not current rankings or rules. Practice cases are fictional.

A teacher supplies a training signal

Hinton, Vinyals and Dean’s 2015 paper develops compression from an ensemble into a single model. The broader teacher–student idea asks what information one model supplies to another. It does not require the student to copy the teacher’s architecture, and it does not by itself specify how the teacher was accessed.

In a response-based workflow, prompts go to a teacher; selected answers become examples used to train a student. In probability matching, the student learns from a teacher’s distribution over outputs rather than only a selected answer. Access to those probabilities need not mean access to the teacher’s weights: the interface might expose them. Conversely, a text-only service may not expose the information a particular method needs.

Synthetic data is the wider category. It can supply questions, answers, preferences, tests or candidate examples for filtering. A model that labels human-written examples plays a different role from one that writes every answer. Draw the arrows and name the data at each arrow before estimating impact.

A mechanism does not establish an effect size

Consider fictional tutor Willow and student Juniper. Juniper improves after receiving Willow’s explanations, but the team also doubles training time and changes the base checkpoint. The result is evidence of improvement in the complete intervention, not an estimate of Willow’s contribution. A before-and-after score cannot separate those changes.

Start a stronger comparison from the same base checkpoint. Hold training budget, evaluation tasks and selection rules as constant as practical. Compare teacher-generated examples with a specified alternative, such as human-authored examples under an explicit budget. Use held-out tasks and repeated training runs where feasible. Record both quality and the cost of acquiring, filtering and verifying data.

Different controls answer different questions. Equal training compute estimates the value of the data under a fixed training budget. Equal total expenditure includes the price of creating the examples. If teacher outputs cost more, a cheaper baseline may buy more training. Neither comparison is inherently dishonest; silently switching between them is misleading.

A documented training case

DeepSeek-R1’s January 2025 report distinguishes R1-Zero, trained with RL from a base model, from R1’s staged process with cold-start data and further training. It also describes distilling reasoning behaviour into smaller models. These author-reported methods rebut a blanket claim that reasoning-related behaviour cannot be transferred. They do not demonstrate which undisclosed data every other laboratory used, or establish a universal amount of uplift.

The important distinction is between transferring useful behaviour and reproducing an entire research process. A student may learn from a teacher’s successful outputs without duplicating all its capabilities or its training history. Treat the resulting evaluation as bounded evidence about the tested student.

Optional depth: on-policy data and reinforcement learning

Agarwal and colleagues’ on-policy distillation study trains a student on sequences it generates, with teacher feedback. Its abstract explicitly describes integration with RL fine-tuning. This is why “distillation versus RL” is a false universal dichotomy. The source supports a method combination; it does not show that all RL pipelines depend on external teachers.

A static teacher dataset and feedback on student-generated sequences expose the student to different distributions. A controlled study should specify which distribution, loss and feedback are being compared. A test that changes all three cannot attribute the outcome to a single design choice.

An allegation needs careful attribution

In its 23 February 2026 disclosure, Anthropic alleged that DeepSeek, Moonshot and MiniMax used fraudulent access to collect Claude outputs for training. This is primary evidence of Anthropic’s report, not an independent adjudication of the named laboratories’ conduct or proof of a specific gain in their final models. Request volume alone cannot establish that gain.

The technical question is what information was transferred. The permission question concerns the actual access, terms and conduct. The causal question concerns what changed because of the transfer. Evidence supporting one does not automatically settle the others. When a report supplies only observations from one side, state that limitation without assuming either that all allegations are proved or that they are false.

Intermediate text is not a complete account

A reasoning trace is generated text. Its presence does not establish that it faithfully reveals every internal computation, and its absence does not establish that a model used no additional computation. To test whether traces help a student, compare against an answer-only condition under a stated budget. Avoid assuming that a larger collection is automatically better: quality, selection and evaluation leakage matter.

Lambert’s objection and the competing concern

In “The distillation panic” (4 May 2026), Nathan Lambert objects to using a broad training-method name as a synonym for abusive access. He argues that this framing can damage legitimate research and diffusion. The counterargument is that access abuse and capability transfer can still create legitimate commercial or security concerns. A useful policy discussion specifies the conduct and the harm rather than trying to resolve everything with a label.

Neither the routine use of a method nor its potential misuse answers whether a particular training run was authorised. Return to the release and service documents from Chapter 1, then separate a conduct claim from a capability claim.
