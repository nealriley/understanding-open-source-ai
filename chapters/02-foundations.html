Why Anyone Builds Open Models

Training a frontier model costs hundreds of millions of dollars. Giving the result away looks irrational until you notice that Google gave away Android, Meta gave away its data-centre designs, and IBM paid $34 billion for a Linux company. This chapter explains the four distinct motives behind open models, and why the mix of motives has shifted from principle to strategy.

What you will be able to do

  • Apply Bill Gurley's "open source strategy" lens (commoditise, neutralise, standardise) to AI.
  • Reconstruct Zuckerberg's Linux argument and identify where it broke down for Meta.
  • Explain Catalini's claim that openness changes the direction of innovation, not the level.
  • Distinguish the strategic, economic, scientific and values cases, and say which actors hold which.

1. Four motives, four kinds of actor

Lambert's March 2026 essay "What comes next with open models" makes a point that is easy to miss: "few companies have genuine economic incentives to build open models; most do so for influence or principle." Sorting the field by motive clarifies who will keep releasing and who will stop.

2. Open source as strategy: Gurley's six cases

Bill Gurley's May 2026 essay "From Open Source Software to Open Source Strategy" is the best single account of why profit-seeking companies give software away. Twenty-seven years after his 1999 piece on the rising impact of open source, his thesis is that open source "has evolved beyond a development methodology into a strategic corporate weapon". Most plays are defensive: thwart a threat, erode a competitor's pricing power, or align an industry on a standard nobody owns.

CaseYearThreat being counteredOutcome

Overture Maps2022Google Maps dominanceMeta, Microsoft, Uber, Esri all migrated

Gurley then applies the lens to AI. The frontier is closed and American (OpenAI, Anthropic); the open frontier is Chinese (DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi, GLM), a fact Demis Hassabis has publicly acknowledged; the closest Western open contender is Mistral; and Meta, the company that wrote the strategy playbook, has "retreated": Llama 4 Behemoth was shelved, the April 2026 Muse Spark model was closed, and Zuckerberg has said Meta will not release superintelligence openly. His warning is that a single regulatory cycle restricting Chinese open models "conveniently" leaves only US closed "cathedrals", and that by 2030 the six billion people outside the US would default to Chinese open models anyway.

Key ideaOpen source strategy needs a neutral referee. Every durable case in Gurley's list involved a foundation (Linux Foundation, CNCF, Apache) that stopped the sponsor from recapturing the project. AI has no equivalent. That is one reason Meta could reverse course in a year, and one reason the ATOM Project (Chapter 5) argues for institution-building rather than just more model releases.

3. The Linux analogy and where it broke

Zuckerberg's July 2024 letter "Open Source AI Is the Path Forward", published with Llama 3.1 405B ("the first frontier-level open source AI model"), is the clearest statement of the strategic case from the inside. The argument has three parts:

  1. Good for developers: train on your own data, avoid lock-in, protect sensitive information, cut costs (running 405B was claimed to be ~50% cheaper than GPT-4o).
  2. Good for Meta: Meta does not sell model access, so open weights cost it no revenue; open weights prevent a repeat of being trapped inside Apple's platform rules; the Open Compute Project saved billions by the same logic.
  3. Good for the world: wider access, less concentration of power, more safety through transparency, and a democratic ecosystem that out-competes adversaries rather than one that tries to lock them out.

The historical analogy was Unix versus Linux: proprietary Unix variants lost to an open kernel that was cheaper, modifiable, and had a better ecosystem. The analogy is strong on economics and weak on one point. Linux was never "the best operating system at the frontier"; it won on cost and adaptability, which is precisely the position open models hold in 2026 (Chapter 6). What changed for Meta was not the argument but the strategy: once Meta decided it wanted to compete at the closed frontier for its own products, the calculus Zuckerberg described no longer applied.

4. The economics: direction, not level

Christian Catalini's August 2026 essay "Some Simple Economics of Open versus Closed AI" brings the innovation-economics literature to bear. Its central move is to say that openness affects the direction of investment rather than its level, citing Petra Moser's study of 15,000 inventions from 1851–1876 exhibitions, which found patent systems changed where inventors focused, not how much they invented.

Several findings from the literature transfer directly:

  • Ideas are non-excludable. Once weights are shared, the marginal cost of copying is zero (Arrow, Nelson). This is the tension between incentivising creation and maximising diffusion.
  • Complementary assets capture value, not the IP. Surveys consistently rank patents low among the ways firms protect innovation; lead time, learning, secrecy and complementary assets rank high. AI labs behave accordingly: they lock in customers through the application layer (Catalini names Claude Cowork, Claude Tag and ChatGPT computer use).
  • Cumulativeness favours openness. Genes sequenced by Celera under IP restrictions attracted 20–30% less follow-on research; AT&T's forced transistor licensing raised inventive activity 17% in five years; accessible biotech inputs boosted cumulativeness by 57–135%.
  • Training runs are only 10–23% of AI R&D compute. The rest is experimentation, which openness accelerates.
  • Innovators capture roughly 2.2% of the social surplus they create. The rest goes to users and follow-on innovators, which is why the diffusion side of the tradeoff is so heavily weighted.

Catalini also flags that the safety calculus is domain-specific: cyber defence is distributed, so openness helps defenders; biology's bottleneck is physical synthesis, so closed models offer less protection than they seem to. Chapter 3 picks this up.

DebateDo enterprises actually want open weights? Catalini reports that enterprises increasingly want to own the "weights of production", meaning models tuned on their proprietary data and tacit knowledge, as protection against being disintermediated by a frontier lab. Thomson Reuters' August 2026 launch of an in-house model built on Qwen for about $40M is a case in point. Lambert's counter-observation (Chapter 9) is that the market for specialised small open models is real but "largely unmet", because most open labs chase the general frontier instead.

5. The scientific and values case from Ai2

Nathan Lambert's October 2024 essay "Why I Build Open Language Models" is the personal statement of someone whose lab (Ai2) sits in the "scientific" row of the table. His reasons are not commercial:

  • Power. Open models reduce the concentration of a technology that increasingly mediates people's relationships, not just their tools.
  • Security through inspection. Community oversight catches problems that a closed lab's red team may not.
  • Regulatory access. Policymakers need "direct access to systems" rather than company-run demonstrations.
  • Evidence over prediction. The predicted harms from open models, "disinformation, biosecurity risks, phishing", have in his reading "been disproven" by events since 2023.

He is candid about the cost. Ai2 was "years behind" closed labs on RLHF, its best preference dataset was over a year old, and much of the work is "white rice research": foundational, unglamorous, enabling. That candour is what makes the ATOM Report and the gap measurements in Chapter 6 credible; the same author documents both the case for open models and the size of their disadvantage.

6. The national motive

The fourth motive is where 2026 differs most from 2024. China's 14th and 15th Five-Year Plans enshrine open source as national strategy; the 2026 Government Work Report calls for Chinese AI models to lead the global open-source ecosystem; and Lambert's Kimi K3 essay notes that Xi Jinping used a World AI Conference address to commit the country explicitly to open-source AI. On the US side, the ATOM Project and the Lambert–Xu essay "Banning Open Source AI Would Be A Mistake" (June 2026) make the mirror-image argument: education, innovation and competition are "core American values", over 90% of global software already depends on open source, and the current concentration between Anthropic and OpenAI is itself a competition problem. Chapter 5 tells the story in full.

Think about it: which motive is most durable?

Strategic openness lasts only as long as the strategy (Meta). Commercial openness tends to close at the top as revenue arrives (Z.ai's flagship was closed in 2024 before DeepSeek changed the calculus; Alibaba kept Qwen Max closed until 2026). Scientific openness is durable but underfunded. National openness depends on the state's continued interest. The safest bet for a builder in 2026 is probably an open model backed by more than one of these motives at once, which is one way to read the Chinese labs' position.
